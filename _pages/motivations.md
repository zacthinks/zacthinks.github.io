Much like a child who incessantly asks 'why?', I have always been driven by the search for the _general_; rather than solving a local problem, I would be compelled solve the general case, even if it takes more time. This impulse to look past the immediate question toward an underlying structure has shaped all of my intellectual life.

One of the most general lines of inquiry is how we go about conducting inquiry itself. While philosophers have approached this question for millennia under the label of 'epistemology', or the study of knowledge and justification, epistemological investigations have traditionally been individualistic. However, starting in the second half of the 20th century, philosophers, sociologists, and historians began to argue that knowledge and justification are deeply and irreducibly social--we rely on testimony, defer to experts, and are influenced by our social positions. These threads led to what is called the _social turn in epistemology_ as a more comprehensive research program. 

It is within this context of social epistemology that I locate the main subject of my curiosity: _research_. I study research as a _human activity_, a _social institution_, and an _organizing concept_. Some questions arise from this framing: 

- To what extent is research shaped by human motives, limitations, and social dynamics—and is there something beyond the human element that could ground its epistemic authority? 
- How do research communities negotiate legitimacy and decide what counts as valid research, especially in the context of disagreements? 
- What should we mean by research, and how might refining the concept improve how we conduct research and the role it plays in society? 

Pursuing these questions naturally led me to _methodological disputes_, since understanding how researchers determine and negotiate the rules and norms of their inquiry is a large part of understanding the epistemic nature of research. In particular, _education_, as a professional field tasked with the onerous task of investigating how future generations should be shaped, serves as a vivid case for understanding relationship between research and society.

_Computer-assisted text analysis (CATA)_ is another natural path that follows from this line of inquiry. If we are to study research as an epistemic institution, scholarly texts are an invaluable source of data, and given how massive research is today, this data often exists at a scale beyond what close reading alone can support. Thus, understanding how computational tools can assist text analysis without displacing the interpretive practices that give research its meaning is deeply valuable. At the same time, the history of CATA and how underrepresented it is in the social sciences reflects many of the methodological divisions I study: longstanding tensions between what is traditionally called qualitative and quantitative research has led to artificial methodological camps that exclude alternatives that don't neatly fit in either category. Thus, computer-assisted text analysis is both a practical necessity for studying research at scale and a case study in the very methodological dynamics I examine.
