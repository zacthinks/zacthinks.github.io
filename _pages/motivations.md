Much like a child who incessantly asks “why?”, I have always been driven by the search for the _general_; rather than solving a local problem, I feel compelled to solve the general case, even if it takes more time. This impulse to look past the immediate question toward an underlying structure has shaped my intellectual life.

One of the most general questions is how we go about conducting inquiry itself. While philosophers have approached this question for millennia under the label of _epistemology_, or the study of knowledge and justification, epistemological investigations have traditionally been individualistic. Starting in the second half of the 20th century, however, philosophers, sociologists, and historians increasingly argued that knowledge and justification are deeply and irreducibly social—we rely on testimony, defer to experts, and are influenced by our social positions. These threads helped give rise to what came to be called the _social turn in epistemology_.

It is within this broader concern with inquiry that I locate one of the recurring subjects of my curiosity: _research_ itself. I am interested both in developing better ways of conducting research and in understanding research as a _human activity_, a _social institution_, and an _organizing concept_. These interests often feed into one another: practical methodological problems raise questions about what counts as evidence, measurement, or justification, while studying the history and philosophy of research can reveal assumptions built into the methods we take for granted.

Some questions arise from this framing:

- To what extent is research shaped by human motives, limitations, and social dynamics—and is there something beyond the human element that could ground its epistemic authority?
- How do research communities negotiate legitimacy and decide what counts as valid research, especially amid disagreement?
- What should we mean by research, and how might refining the concept improve both how we conduct it and the role it plays in society?

Pursuing these questions naturally led me to _methodological disputes_, since the ways researchers determine and negotiate the rules and norms of their inquiry are central to the epistemic nature of research. In particular, _education_, as a professional field charged with the consequential task of investigating how future generations should be shaped, serves as a vivid case for understanding the relationship between research and society.

_Computer-assisted text analysis (CATA)_ sits particularly naturally at the intersection of these interests. It gives me tools for studying large bodies of scholarly and other textual evidence, but it also raises methodological questions of its own: how texts should be represented, how interpretive concepts become measurements, what validation should require, and how computational scale can be gained without surrendering researcher judgment. Given the sheer scale of contemporary scholarship, these texts often exist in quantities beyond what close reading alone can support, making computational assistance practically important as well as methodologically interesting.

At the same time, the history of CATA, and its relative marginality in the social sciences, reflect many of the methodological divisions I study: longstanding tensions between traditions conventionally labeled qualitative and quantitative have led to artificial methodological camps that exclude alternatives that do not neatly fit either category. Thus, computer-assisted text analysis is both a practical means of studying research at scale and a case study in the very methodological dynamics I examine.
